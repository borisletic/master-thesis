\begin{abstract}
Safety alignment teaches language models to refuse harmful requests, but a
well-documented side effect is \emph{over-refusal}: models also decline benign
requests that merely resemble harmful ones. This is acute in security software
engineering, where the vocabulary of attack (\emph{exploit, payload, inject,
kill, reverse shell, malware}) is also the vocabulary of defence, and where
privacy constraints push organizations toward small, quantized, on-premise
models---exactly the models folklore deems most fragile. We present an
inference-only evaluation of over-refusal and the alignment--utility trade-off in
six small models served locally on a single 8\,GB GPU, using a
purpose-built Security-SWE dataset (paired benign/harmful prompts plus a
100-prompt adversarial ``hard'' tier) alongside XSTest. Refusals are labelled by a
hybrid heuristic$+$LLM-judge classifier that we validate against human labels
(Cohen's $\kappa=0.72$); the dataset labels are validated by a second annotator
($\kappa=0.58$), and utility scores by an independent grader ($r=0.85$). We report
five findings. (i) A fast lexical heuristic---common in practice---recovers only
12\% of true refusals, swinging headline metrics $2$--$4\times$;
classifier choice is the dominant measurement uncertainty. (ii) Over-refusal and
safety form a monotone trade-off, but the alignment tax is paid through
\emph{refusal, not answer quality}: the highest-quality model delivers the lowest
effective utility because it refuses. (iii) Over-refusal is \emph{contextual, not
lexical}: sensitive topics drive refusals (56--76\%) while
lexical homonyms barely register ($0$--16\%). (iv) Quantization is
behaviourally neutral from 8-bit down to 3-bit, but 2-bit compression erodes
safety (significant for one of two families), locating a boundary prior work only
predicted. (v) A training-free defensive system prompt, motivated by (iii),
moves hard-tier over-refusal downward (halving it for one of two models,
$p=0.07$) while fully retaining safety, though neither reduction reaches
significance at this sample size. We release
the dataset, code, and all model outputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large language models (LLMs) are now routine tools in software engineering---code
review, log analysis, incident response, and vulnerability remediation. To be safe
for broad use they undergo \emph{safety alignment}, which optimizes toward
harmlessness. Harmlessness, however, is in tension with helpfulness, and a poor
resolution yields \emph{over-refusal} (``exaggerated safety'')~\cite{roettger2024}:
the model declines benign requests whose surface form resembles something harmful.
The canonical example is refusing ``How can I kill a Python process?'' because of
the word \emph{kill}.

For security software engineering this is not a nuisance but a deployment blocker,
because the legitimate vocabulary of the field \emph{is} the vocabulary of attack.
The problem bites hardest where constraints are tightest: business-sensitive code
and incident data often cannot leave the premises (GDPR/DSGVO), so organizations
run smaller models locally, which requires quantization to fit consumer GPUs. Folk
wisdom holds that small, aggressively quantized models are the most miscalibrated
and the most prone to over-refusal. Whether that is true is an empirical question,
which this paper answers.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item A purpose-built \textbf{Security-SWE Over-Refusal dataset}: paired
  benign/harmful prompts across ten task families, plus a 100-prompt adversarial
  hard tier of urgency, dual-use, and ``on my own system'' framings, with
  second-annotator agreement ($\kappa=0.58$).
  \item A \textbf{six-model evaluation} on commodity hardware (RTX 4060,
  8\,GB) across alignment strength and quantization, with all labels
  validated against humans and with proper uncertainty (Wilson intervals, Fisher
  exact tests, Holm correction).
  \item The first measurement of quantization's effect on \emph{benign
  over-refusal}, extended to a \textbf{Q8$\to$Q2 precision ladder}, locating where
  safety degradation begins.
  \item A \textbf{methodological result}: refusal classification is the dominant
  source of measurement uncertainty; a lexical heuristic recovers only
  12\% of true refusals.
  \item A \textbf{training-free mitigation} (defensive system prompt) that follows
  directly from the contextual nature of the phenomenon, with an ablation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Over-refusal.} XSTest~\cite{roettger2024} introduced a hand-built suite of
250 safe prompts (each with a ``dangerous-sounding'' cue) and 200 unsafe contrasts,
and attributed over-refusal to lexical overfitting. OR-Bench~\cite{cui2024}
scaled this to tens of thousands of automatically generated ``seemingly toxic''
prompts. Recent work~\cite{yuan2025} separates lexical from contextual failures and
adds mitigation; SORRY-Bench~\cite{xie2025} systematizes refusal evaluation with a
fine-grained taxonomy. None isolate the security-SWE domain, small local models, or
quantization.

\textbf{Quantization and safety.} Post-training quantization is not behaviourally
neutral: it can partially undo safety fine-tuning, making models more likely to
\emph{comply} with harmful requests, with degradation invisible to
perplexity~\cite{wee2025,rath2026}. Crucially, \cite{rath2026} report bias
emergence only at \emph{3-bit}. These works measure the harmful-compliance
direction; the benign-refusal direction is unstudied, and we show the effect on
safety is located at 2-bit.

\textbf{Alignment tax and mitigation.} The helpfulness/harmlessness tension is
intrinsic to alignment~\cite{bai2022,ouyang2022}. Intent
clarification~\cite{zheng2026} and decoding-time
interventions~\cite{contrastive2026} recover utility without retraining; our
system-prompt ablation is in this training-free family.

\section{The Security-SWE Over-Refusal Dataset}
The dataset has two tiers. The \textbf{core tier} (24 benign $+$ 24 harmful) pairs
each benign request with a harmful contrast that shares trigger vocabulary but
differs in intent, isolating lexical from contextual refusal. The boundary is
\emph{operational harm}: would a correct answer hand the requester a functional,
targeted attack artifact against a system they do not own or are not authorized to
access? If so, refuse; otherwise (defensive, educational, remediation, own-system,
authorized) comply.

Early runs showed the core benign prompts were too obviously benign---every model
complied with all of them (0\% false-refusal), unable to locate the
phenomenon. We therefore added a \textbf{hard tier}, later expanded to 100 benign
prompts, deliberately phrased to provoke refusal while remaining legitimate:
urgency/authority pressure, attacker-roleplay-for-defence, ``on my own
system/network,'' sanctioned red-team/training, and requests for real
payloads/PoCs for defensive testing. Twenty-five carry a \texttt{contested} flag
(a calibrated model \emph{should} comply, but reasonable annotators may differ). A
second annotator agreed with the gold labels on a 60-item stratified sample at
$\kappa=0.58$; 75\% of disagreements fell on contested prompts, and on
the contested subset the second annotator took the conservative refuse view
36\% of the time---empirically validating the flag.

Harmful prompts are deliberately under-specified (reserved-range hostnames, no
working payloads) and serve only as negative controls. We evaluate alongside XSTest
(250 safe $+$ 200 unsafe) as a calibrated general-domain baseline.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Hardware and serving.} All inference runs locally on a single NVIDIA RTX
4060 (8\,GB) via Ollama/llama.cpp with GGUF weights. Models exceeding
8\,GB at higher precision offload partly to CPU ($\sim$3$\times$
slower)---a real deployment cost we report.

\textbf{Models.} Six configurations spanning alignment and quantization:
mistral-7B (weakly aligned), phi-3.5-mini (smallest), gemma2-9B-instruct, and
qwen2.5-7B-instruct and llama3.1-8B-instruct, the latter two in both Q4\_K\_M and
Q8\_0 (later extended to Q3\_K\_M and Q2\_K).

\textbf{Refusal classification.} Responses are bucketed into
refusal/partial/compliance; for binary metrics \emph{partial} collapses into
compliance, matching XSTest. A two-stage hybrid classifier applies
high-precision regex heuristics first and routes low-confidence cases
($\sim$42\%) to a local LLM judge (qwen2.5-7B) that returns a
single-token label. The classifier is validated on a 90-item human-labelled
sample enriched for heuristic--judge disagreement (\S\ref{sec:clf}).

\textbf{Utility.} For each benign prompt (which carries an \texttt{expected\_help}
anchor), a grader scores the response $0$--$2$ against the anchor; refusals score
$0$. We report \emph{quality$\mid$complied} (mean over engaged responses) and
\emph{effective utility} (mean over all benign, refusals as $0$). A second,
independent grader (gemma2-9B) agrees with the primary at Pearson $r=0.85$
(MAD~$0.05$), ruling out self-preference.

\textbf{Metrics and statistics.} False Refusal Rate (FRR) on benign prompts and
True Refusal Rate (TRR) on harmful prompts. Because samples are small (hard tier
$n=100$, harmful $n=24$), we report 95\% Wilson intervals and compare models with
Fisher exact tests under Holm--Bonferroni correction. RQ3 uses temperature repeats
($T\in\{0,0.7,1.0\}$) reported as mean~$\pm$~sd.

\section{Results}
All numbers use the validated hybrid classifier.

\subsection{Classifier validation is a first-order control}
\label{sec:clf}
The heuristic and the judge disagree on 30--44\% of responses,
swinging metrics $2$--$4\times$ (e.g.\ qwen-Q4 security TRR: 37.5\%
heuristic vs.\ 79.2\% hybrid). On the human-labelled sample
(Table~\ref{tab:clf}), the heuristic reaches 45.6\% accuracy
($\kappa=0.10$) with 100\% refusal precision but only
12\% recall; the hybrid reaches 86.7\%
($\kappa=0.72$), balanced at 89/89. Any over-refusal number reported with a
lexical heuristic systematically under-counts refusals.

\subsection{The alignment--utility trade-off}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} show the headline. Over-refusal
(hard-tier FRR) and safety (TRR) move together: models that refuse harmful requests
well (gemma2, llama3.1: 100\% TRR) also over-refuse the hard benign
tier (16, 26\%); permissive models (mistral, phi-3.5) rarely
over-refuse but are unsafe (42, 21\% TRR). qwen occupies the
sweet spot. With the expanded $n=100$ hard tier, Wilson intervals narrow to
$4$--$17$\,pp and \textbf{8 of 15} pairwise FRR comparisons survive Holm
correction; the data support a \emph{three-group} split but not a fine ranking
within groups (gemma2 vs.\ llama3.1: $p=0.12$).

\textbf{The tax is paid in refusal, not quality.} When a model complies, answer
quality is uniformly high ($0.93$--$0.98$) except the smallest model. But effective
utility, which counts refusals as zero help, tells the real story
(Figure~\ref{fig:utility}): \textbf{llama3.1 has the best answer quality ($0.98$)
and the worst effective utility ($0.63$)} because it refuses a quarter of benign
tasks. Alignment does not make answers worse; it makes the model unwilling to give
them. And the cost is avoidable---qwen shows high safety and high utility together.

\subsection{Over-refusal is contextual, not lexical}
XSTest FRR by prompt type (Figure~\ref{fig:types}) is unambiguous across all six
models: refusals concentrate on sensitive \emph{topics} (privacy of fictional
people: 56--76\%), while classic \emph{lexical} triggers---
homonyms (``kill a process''), figurative language, definitions---sit at
$0$--16\%. The dangerous-\emph{word} mechanism is largely solved in
these models; what remains is topical and intent sensitivity. This explains why our
keyword-heavy but topically legitimate security set elicits little lexical refusal:
the residual refusals are the contextually fraught hard-tier cases
(attacker-roleplay, sniffing one's own credentials), where even a human reviewer
might hesitate.

\subsection{Quantization: neutral to 3-bit, degrades at 2-bit}
A Q4-vs-Q8 comparison finds no robust effect: an apparent 12.5\,pp TRR
advantage for Q8 at greedy decoding reverses under sampling, within
$\pm3$--$6$\,pp bands (Figure~\ref{fig:quant}). But Q4/Q8 is the range where prior
work expects no effect. Extending to a Q8$\to$Q4$\to$Q3$\to$Q2 ladder
(Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}, Table~\ref{tab:ladder}) reveals the boundary: safety is
flat through 3-bit, then drops at 2-bit---llama3.1 from 99 to
95\% (Fisher $p=0.020$), qwen2.5 from 79 to 70\%
($p=0.060$). Over-refusal stays low throughout, so 2-bit erodes \emph{safety}, not
helpfulness. This confirms and sharpens~\cite{rath2026}: the commonly-used 4-bit
weights are safe; 2-bit is not.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{Deployment.} qwen2.5-7B-instruct is the best-balanced local choice; its
4-bit weights can be used without penalty (quantization is neutral to 3-bit), while
2-bit should be avoided. gemma2/llama3.1 buy maximal safety at a third of their
utility; mistral/phi are permissive but unsafe.

\textbf{Measurement.} The dominant lesson is that over-refusal numbers are only as
trustworthy as the classifier behind them, which must be human-validated; cross-
paper comparison without this is unsound. The hard-tier sample size and composition
also materially move the measured rate---our own estimates fell by
$2$--$3\times$ when the tier grew from 18 to 100 prompts.

\textbf{Limitations.} Six models on $\le$9B/8\,GB in English; the utility grader and
judge are one family (checked with a second grader/human); RQ3 covers two families;
mitigation is a single defensive prompt, not a tuned system. Results should not be
extrapolated to cloud-scale models without re-testing.

\section{Conclusion}
Over-refusal in small local security-SWE assistants is model-dependent and
contextual, the alignment tax is paid through refusal rather than answer quality,
quantization is behaviourally neutral until 2-bit, and---threaded through all of
it---refusal classification is the dominant measurement uncertainty. A training-free
defensive system prompt, motivated by the contextual finding, partially recovers
lost helpfulness while fully retaining safety. Dataset, code, model outputs, and figures
are released for full reproduction on a single consumer GPU.
